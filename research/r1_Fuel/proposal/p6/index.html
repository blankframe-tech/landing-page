r1_Fuel BlankFrame Research
Research plan · updated 1 October 2026

P6 — Whose Mobility? (Gender Module / Paired Panel)

Core question: How does the fuel shock reshape gender inequality inside the household — who cuts back first, whose mobility is curtailed, what obstacles women specifically face in coping — and is that gap driven partly by women simply knowing less about the shock, or caring about it differently, than men in the same household?

Design

Element Detail
Method CATI, paired-respondent design (the female adult and the male adult in the same household, interviewed separately)
Sample A random ~600–800 household subsample drawn from P1’s existing sample and stratification (adds the opposite-gender second respondent to each), plus a standalone booster of ~300–400 women-headed households and RMG women night-shift workers, who are undercounted by P1’s single-respondent design even with its 40% female-respondent quota
Coverage Same six sites as P1 (Dhaka, Chattogram, Gazipur/Narayanganj, Rangpur, Rajshahi, one coastal district), with the RMG booster concentrated in Gazipur/Narayanganj
Waves Shares P1’s calendar — W0 now → W1 Nov → W2 Jan → W3 Mar → W4 Jun 2027 — so gender gaps can be tracked across the same shock cycle, not just measured once
Each wave ~10–12 min per respondent (two respondents per linked household, called separately)
Interviewer requirement Female respondents must be interviewed by women interviewers — standard practice for gendered/mobility content in Bangladesh, and a real cost and staffing constraint P1 does not carry
Budget ~$20–35k incremental if piggybacked on P1’s sample/waves/survey-firm contract (recommended); ~$30–45k if fielded standalone

Why a separate module, not just another item in P1

P1’s Module 5 already asks one respondent “who in the household ate less, who gave up mobility first” (../p1_fuel_shock_panel/instrument_w0.md §10). That is a single person’s perception of the whole household — usually reported by whoever picked up the phone, more often the male household head given P1’s own stated risk that phone surveys under-represent women. P6 asks the same underlying question of both adults independently, which turns a self-report into a measurable intra-household discordance: does the woman report giving up more mobility than the man reports her giving up, or than she is willing to say to a stranger while the household is in earshot? That gap — not just the level — is the finding P1 alone cannot produce.

Core modules (each wave)

  • Awareness and salience test — an objective knowledge check (did fuel prices rise, roughly by how much, when, and why), not just self-reported concern, asked of both respondents to compute a gender gap in what people actually know, not just what they say they think about it
  • Mobility diary (7-day recall) — trips by purpose (work, market, health/clinic, children’s school, social/religious), whether the trip happened, was shortened, delayed, cancelled, or reassigned to someone else in the household; mode and cost change; whether a chaperone is now required where one wasn’t before; any safety incident or harassment on a cheaper or more crowded mode
  • Gendered coping and obstacles — unpaid-labour substitution (time spent gathering firewood/biomass instead of buying kerosene or LPG), forgone healthcare specifically for reproductive and antenatal visits, girls’ school attendance risk, and structural obstacles to compensation — NID and mobile-money accounts (the access route to the Family Card, per P1’s Module 9) are disproportionately registered in the male household head’s name
  • Workplace and commute (RMG booster subsample) — commute mode, cost and time change for women on factory night shifts; perceived safety; absenteeism; whether transport-allowance demands are being raised at factory level (this is A5 in research_proposals.md)
  • Bargaining and decision-making — who decides where the household cuts back, asked of both respondents, and whether that decision-making pattern has shifted since the hike
  • Embedded vignette (contingent behaviour) — “if bus/rickshaw fare rose by [randomised amount], would you still make this trip?” asked about a market trip and a clinic trip, to both respondents in the pair, producing a directly comparable mobility-cost threshold by gender rather than a subjective mobility question alone

Analysis

Within-household paired comparison (female vs. male respondent, same household, same wave) on awareness, reported mobility loss, and cost thresholds — this is the design’s main analytic leverage over a single-respondent survey. Panel fixed effects across waves, as in P1. Compare the RMG-booster subsample against the general sample on commute and safety items.

What we are asking from reviewers

  • Is there an existing validated mobility/agency module (BIGD, BRAC, or another Bangladesh survey house) we should adapt instead of building the vignette and mobility-diary items from scratch, for comparability with prior work?
  • Is the paired-respondent design (asking to speak with a specific second household member by name) going to trigger refusals or suspicion in a way a single-respondent call does not — and if so, how have other Bangladesh household surveys handled recruiting a named second respondent from the same household?
  • Are there structural conditions specific to women’s mobility and coping that this design still misses?

Recent Context (September 2026 Perspective)

The gendered stakes of this hike are sharper than the aggregate numbers suggest. RMG employment — widely reported as majority-women, though this project has not independently verified a current figure and P6’s own screener (§L2/W-module) should record respondent gender directly rather than lean on an external share — carries a minimum wage frozen since December 2023 (README §2), so the same Tk 260–300/month realised bus-fare increase that A5 identifies for RMG workers generally lands on a wage that has not moved while it happens. The National Pay Scale that insulated ~33 lakh public employees from the hike (see p1_content.md’s recent-context note) is overwhelmingly a formal, urban, disproportionately male benefit; it does nothing for the informal and RMG workforce where most women in the labour force actually are. And the Family Card, the government’s stated mitigation tool, runs on NID and mobile-money registration that is frequently held in the male household head’s name — meaning the mechanism meant to cushion the shock may be structurally harder for women to access even inside a household that qualifies. P6 is built to put numbers on all three of these gaps, wave by wave, rather than leave them as background assumptions.
